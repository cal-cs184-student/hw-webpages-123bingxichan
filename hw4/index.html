Overview

In this project I implemented a cloth simulator using a mass-spring model. The cloth is represented as a grid of point masses connected by structural, shearing, and bending springs. Each simulation step accumulates external forces, spring forces, Verlet integration, spring length correction, collision response, and self-collision correction. I also implemented the shading portion of the assignment, including diffuse shading, Blinn-Phong shading, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, and mirror shading. The main lesson was that the visual quality depends on both the physics parameters and the rendering model, as high stiffness can reduce stretching but can introduce jitter, while shader effects such as bump mapping can add detail without changing geometry.

Part 1: Masses and Springs

I built the cloth as a row-major grid of point masses. For horizontal cloth, each mass lies in the x-z plane with a fixed y value; for vertical cloth, each mass lies in the x-y plane with a small random z offset so the cloth does not begin perfectly flat. The pinned list from the scene file marks which masses should stay fixed. After creating the masses, I connected neighboring masses with springs: structural springs connect immediate horizontal and vertical neighbors, shearing springs connect diagonal neighbors, and bending springs connect masses two grid steps apart horizontally or vertically. These springs store their rest lengths at construction time, which are later used by Hooke's law and the Provot length constraint.

Part 2: Simulation via Numerical Integration

For each simulation step I first compute the total external force on each point mass, mainly gravity scaled by the mass of a grid point. Then I add spring forces using Hooke's law. Bending springs use a weaker stiffness, 0.2 times the main stiffness, because they are meant to resist folding gently rather than make the cloth rigid. I update positions with Verlet integration: the new position depends on the current position, previous position, damping, and acceleration over the time step. Pinned masses are skipped. After integration, I apply collision handling and then enforce Provot's constraint so no spring can stretch beyond 110 percent of its rest length in a single step.

Low ks makes the springs weaker, so the cloth stretches and sags more before settling. High ks makes the springs resist stretching more strongly; the cloth holds its shape better, but the motion can look more energetic or jittery because the forces become much larger.

Increasing density gives each point mass more mass, so gravity has a stronger visual effect and the cloth tends to sag more deeply. Lower density makes the cloth feel lighter, with less pronounced sag. Damping controls how quickly motion dies out: low damping lets the cloth swing and oscillate for longer, while high damping makes it settle much faster.

Part 3: Handling Collisions with Other Objects

For sphere collisions, I check whether a point mass has moved inside the sphere by comparing its distance from the sphere center to the sphere radius. If it is inside, I project it back to the sphere surface along the radial direction and apply friction by moving only part of the correction from the previous position. For plane collisions, I check whether the point mass crossed the plane between its last position and current position. If it crossed, I compute the intersection point along that path, offset it slightly away from the plane to avoid numerical sticking, and again scale the correction by friction.

Part 4: Handling Self-Collisions

I implemented self-collision using spatial hashing. At the beginning of the self-collision pass, each point mass is placed into a hash bucket based on a 3D box around its position. The box dimensions are based on the cloth grid spacing, with depth set to the larger of the width and height bucket sizes. For each point mass, I only compare it against other point masses in the same bucket. If another point mass is closer than twice the cloth thickness, I compute a correction vector that pushes the current mass away until the pair is separated by the minimum allowed distance. I average all corrections for that mass and divide by the number of simulation steps, which keeps the correction gradual and stable.

With low density, the cloth is lighter and tends to form smoother, broader folds. With high density, the cloth falls more aggressively and produces more compact wrinkles as it collides with itself. Changing ks affects how much the cloth stretches while folding: low ks allows looser, deeper folds, while high ks keeps the cloth tighter and smoother.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program on the GPU is the pair (or small pipeline) of stages you write that turns mesh data into pixels. The vertex shader runs once per vertex: it transforms positions (for example from model space to clip space), and can also output varyings such as world-space position, normal, tangent, and UV coordinates that get interpolated across each triangle. The fragment shader runs once per covered pixel (fragment): it receives those interpolated values and decides the final color, including lighting, textures, and special effects. Together, the vertex stage establishes where the surface is and what per-pixel inputs should be, and the fragment stage interprets that as how the surface responds to light and materials.

Blinn–Phong is a local shading model that approximates how a surface looks under a few light sources. It splits the response into three terms: ambient (a small constant so shadows are not pitch black), diffuse (Lambert-like: brightness grows with how much the surface faces the light), and specular (shiny highlights). Blinn–Phong’s specular uses the halfway vector between the view direction and the light direction instead of the reflected light direction, then raises the dot product with the normal to a shininess power. The lit color is typically the sum of ambient, diffuse, and specular (times material and light colors). Below are the four required breakdowns plus the full model.

Texture mapping with custom texture

For bump vs displacement, I used the same non-default texture for both. Bump mapping perturbs the shading normal using the height map (typically in tangent space), so lighting looks detailed but the underlying triangle mesh does not move; that is why the cloth and sphere can show rich detail without changing silhouette. Displacement mapping uses the height map in the vertex shader to offset vertices along the normal, so real geometry (and silhouettes) can change, but only where the mesh has enough tessellation. Comparing the two visually: bump tends to read as “fine lighting detail,” while displacement reads as “actual bumps and valleys” when the mesh is fine enough. On the sphere, I compared a coarse mesh (-o 16 -a 16) to a fine mesh (-o 128 -a 128): bump still suggests detail on the coarse sphere because normals are faked per pixel, whereas displacement on the coarse sphere looks blocky or muted until the mesh is refined, where displacement becomes much more convincing.
